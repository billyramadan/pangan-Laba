Accurate forecasting of food commodity prices is essential for mitigating economic instability and ensuring na-tional food security. While deep learning has advanced time series forecasting, this study employs the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) architecture for multi-step (30-day) forecasting of daily prices across ten strategic food commodities in Indonesia. The TFT’s performance is compared against benchmark models Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), ARIMA, and Naïve models. The results demonstrate TFT’s supe-rior performance, particularly for commodities with high price variability. For these, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of the best-performing benchmark was higher than TFT’s by 285.6% for bird’s eye chili, 65.7% for shal-lots, 52.8% for red chili, 36.2% for chicken meat, and 29.9% for beef. This work validates the TFT as a powerful tool for developing data-driven policy and more effective price management in Indonesia.
Introduction
Ensuring food security is a critical global challenge, fundamentally linked to economic access and the affordability of staple goods. In emerging economies like Indonesia, this link is especially fragile, with food price inflation posing a direct threat to both macroeconomic sta-bility and the well-being of millions. It is known that an increase in food prices is negatively correlated with purchasing power, and the impact is even worse for the poor, who spend 75% of their household expenditure on food [1]. According to Statistics Indonesia, the consumer price index for the food segment saw approximately 6.9% cumulative increase from 2021 to 2023, indicating there was an increase in food item prices [2]. If this trend continues, it could lead to higher poverty rates and an in-creased risk of food insecurity, posing challenges to achieving SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 2 (Zero Hunger).
To manage this, Indonesian policy focuses on ten items classified as strategic food commodities by The National Strategic Food Price Infor-mation Center: shallots, garlic, rice, red chili, bird’s eye chili, chicken meat, beef, sugar, cooking oil, and chicken eggs. The stability of these specific commodities is considered paramount for controlling inflation and ensuring national food security, which highlights the urgency of developing accurate methods for predicting their prices [3].
The challenge in food price forecasting, however, is magnified by the inherent complexity and uncertainty surrounding food systems. The
price fluctuations of food commodities are the result of a dynamic in-terplay between supply-side shocks such as unpredictable weather pat-terns, natural events, and distribution costs, and demand-side pressures like household income and consumer behaviour [4]. In consequence, these systems are prone to sudden disruptions that can alter their in-ternal dynamics. This growing uncertainty can deter investment in the food production sector, which in turn hampers productivity, lowers ef-ficiency, and threatens long-term sustainability. Additionally, in devel-oping countries where small-scale farmers are the foundation of the agricultural sector, this investment deficit weakens household resilience against economic shocks [5].
Given these concerns, anticipating future problems under this uncer-tainty calls for a robust analysis of past shocks and dynamics to better manage potential trade-offs and respond to unpredictable developments [6]. In this context, the ability to forecast future food prices becomes a crucial tool. Accurate price predictions can assist the government, as pol-icymakers, in formulating targeted interventions, map out target policy areas, and measure the scale of required policies to maintain food price stability [7]. Additionally, price predictions can serve as early warnings for producers, farmers, and traders about price fluctuations, aiding in the development of sales and production strategies to mitigate risks of loss.

This multi-step forecasting task can be approached using two primary strategies: iterative and direct. To address these challenges of architectural robustness and multi-horizon forecasting, this study proposes the Temporal Fusion Trans-former (TFT). Unlike traditional RNNs, TFT utilizes a novel attention mechanism to learn temporal relationships across multiple time scales simultaneously [17]. Crucially, it is specifically designed for multi-horizon forecasting and incorporates features like gating mechanisms to filter out irrelevant noise and variable selection to identify significant predictors, making it theoretically ideal for the volatile and complex nature of commodity price data.
Hence, this study proposes TFT to predict the daily prices of ten strategic food commodities in Indonesia for the next 30 days. The fluc-tuation price of food commodities may depict the complex temporal dependencies. The study aims to develop an accurate prediction model for multi step time series for ten strategic food commodities and com-pares the performance with other deep learning based methods: LSTM, GRU, a classic time series approach: ARIMA, and the basic method: naïve forecasting.
The contributions of this study can be summarized as follows.
The study conducts the first comprehensive application and evalu-ation of the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) for multi-horizon forecasting across ten strategic food commodities in Indonesia. By expanding significantly on prior work that focused on a single com-modity [23], our study provides crucial comparative insights into TFT’s performance across diverse commodity types, thereby estab-lishing a robust benchmark for its utility in volatile food markets
This study develops and validates a high-performance forecasting framework that can serve as a practical decision-support tool for pol-icymakers and supply chain stakeholders in Indonesia. This frame-work provides actionable, multi-horizon price intelligence to support policies aimed at ensuring food security and stabilizing markets.

Proposed model: temporal fusion transformers (TFT)
TFT incorporates self-attention mechanism from Transformer [25] that was originally built for natural language processing application. TFT’s architecture consists of six components which differs from other deep learning models: (a) gating mechanism using Gated Residual Net-work (GRN) that allows the model to perform non-linear processing se-lectively, (b) variable selection networks that can remove any irrelevant inputs, (c) static covariate encoders that extract static metadata’s infor-mation to enhance the temporal features, (d) multi-head attention to process specifically long-term temporal relationships within the inputs,
(e) temporal fusion decoders that includes sequence-to-sequence layer, static enrichment layer, temporal self-attention layer, and position-wise feed-forward layer to learn temporal relationships, and (f) prediction in-tervals by generating percentiles forecast simultaneously. Owing to its strength and model availability, ARIMA is the most frequently used technique for analyzing time-series data and has been shown to produce satisfactory results. 3.2. Experimental settings and compared methods
TFT, LSTM, GRU networks were implemented using Tensorflow’s Keras and Darts libaries. Several key hyperparameters were determined, namely, neurons per layer (hidden size), dropout, and learning rate for all LSTM, GRU, and TFT models, as well as the number of attention heads for TFT. Hyperparameter tuning for the LSTM and GRU models was con-ducted using the Hyperband algorithm [39], while the TFT model em-ployed Optuna for optimization [40]. The number of trials was set to 100 and the process of tuning for TFT was accelerated using NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU. The tuning range for all models is presented in Table 2.
As a commonly used existing method, ARIMA was applied as a base-line model along with Naïve method. In this study direct ARIMA was used. The ARIMA model configuration was selected using the auto_arima function from pmdarima.

Model evaluation is performed using root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). RMSE measures the spread of the residuals. It reflects the average difference between the ex-pected and actual value, while MAPE displays the error’s relative aver-age.

Data preparation and processing
This section begins by detailing the data preparation process, fol-lowed by a thorough explanation of the experimental settings for the TFT, LSTM, GRU, Naïve and ARIMA. Finally, the evaluation method employed to assess forecasting performance is presented.
Data preparation
This study builds on our preliminary work published in [33]. The data for ten commodities were sourced from Indonesia’s National Strate-gic Food Price Information Center website, spanning from 1 August 2017 to 31 August 2024. The national average price is calculated by averag-ing daily prices across 33 provinces in Indonesia. To ensure reliability, only averages from days where at least 30 provinces reported data were included. Any missing values were subsequently imputed using linear interpolation. Descriptive statistics for the dataset, including mean, stan-dard deviation, minimum, median, maximum, and coeﬃcient of varia-tion (CV), are presented in Table 1. The data is split into three parts: training, validation, and testing, with a ratio of 80:10:10 [34–36]. Data from August 2018 to April 2023 is used for training, while data from May to December 2023 is allocated for validation. Testing is conducted on data spanning January to August 2024. The model is trained on the training set, with the validation set incorporated in the training pro-cess to evaluate the model’s performance. Lastly, the testing set is used to assess the model’s predictive ability on unseen data. The data were transformed to a scale of 0 to 1 using min-max normalization to reduce noise and speed up calculations [37,38]. Fig. 4 plots the daily prices for all ten food commodities.

Results and discussion
Comparison of evaluation of the prediction results of the TFT, LSTM, GRU, ARIMA, and naïve models
The forecast results of the TFT, LSTM, GRU, ARIMA, and Naïve is shown in Fig. 5. The graph in Figure 5(g) and 5(h) shows how TFT is the only model able to predict the movement in red chili’s and bird’s eye chili’s price during a period of high volatility in January 2024. The graph clearly shows the actual price undergoing a steep, sustained de-cline. Amidst the tested models, the TFT forecast is unique in its ability to closely mirror this downward trajectory, while other models failed entirely to predict this trend reversal, instead forecasting a flat or even slightly increasing price. This period of price was a direct consequence of shifting market fundamentals in Indonesia. The end of the peak de-mand from the Christmas and New Year holiday season [41], combined with an influx of supply from regions entering the main harvest sea-son, created the strong downward pressure observed. The success of the TFT in this context suggests its architecture can learn and model the complex, event-driven seasonality inherent in agricultural commodity prices. It effectively anticipated the market correction, showcasing its potential as a powerful early warning system.

The results suggest that TFT possesses a comparative advantage in forecasting complex datasets characterized by high variability as well as identifying predictive signals within what might otherwise be considered random noise, where other models may struggle to capture intricate temporal patterns.
Conversely, the commodities where TFT was outperformed by sim-pler models like LSTM and GRU, namely rice and chicken egg, exhibit the opposite characteristics. Based on Table 4, these time series are dom-inated by an exceptionally strong trend component (Trend Strength of 0.986 and 0.860, respectively) and possess a negligible noise compo-nent. In such scenarios, the complexity of TFT may be a significant dis-advantage. The model might be attempting to find intricate, non-linear patterns in a very low-variance residual, leading it to learn spurious correlations and fail to generalize the primary trend–a classic case of overfitting on trend-dominant, low-noise data.
For other commodities like garlic and sugar, the classical ARIMA model provided the most accurate forecasts despite the presence of sig-
nificant noise (Noise Strength of 0.354 and 0.258, respectively). This suggests that the stochastic nature of these particular series, while com-plex, is better captured by the linear autoregressive and moving average components foundational to ARIMA. It highlights that for some struc-tures, the parsimony and specific statistical assumptions of a model like ARIMA provide a more effective safeguard against overfitting than the unconstrained non-linear approach of deep learning models [44].
Fig. 6 shows the percentage of improvement made by TFT com-pared to other models. In commodities that TFT predicted best, such as bird’s eye chili and chili, the number of MAE improvements from Naïve can reach above around 700-800%. When compared against benchmark models, TFT shows its best performance at predicting sugar’s price, giv-ing 233% and 216% improvement from Naïve and ARIMA MAE scores, respectively.
The maximum error gain from TFT is shown to be around 70% against GRU in predicting the shallot’s price. Of all commodities, TFT result was the worst at predicting cooking oil’s price, being only bet-ter than one model, LSTM. TFT utilizes a mechanism that allows one to learn both short-term and long-term temporal dependencies.
The demonstrated superiority of the TFT model in forecasting prices for highly volatile commodities like chili and shallots is of particular importance for macroeconomic management. These commodities are well-documented contributors to volatile food inflation in Indonesia. A reduction in the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for red chili can represent a more reliable signal for policymakers. This allows for proactive mone-tary policy considerations and helps prevent sudden inflationary shocks that can erode national purchasing power.
Comparison of computational time for LSTM and GRU models
Table 5 presents the computation times for training the LSTM, GRU, and TFT models. The LSTM and GRU models were trained on an AMD Ryzen 5 7535HS processor with Radeon Graphics, while the TFT model
was trained using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU. Besides relying on a large dataset to achieve good forecast accuracy, TFT is also known to be computationally expensive [19]. Table 5 shows that GRU and LSTM are 7-8 times faster on average than TFT. The GRU can operate more eﬃciently because its structure combines the input gate and forget gate calculations in the LSTM into the update gate, causing the same function on the LSTM to be performed in a shorter time [14].
Conclusion
The study is concluded as follows. First, TFT provides the best re-sults for price predictions of chicken meat, beef, shallot, red chili and bird’s eye chili. Three of those commodities have a high CV value, which implies that TFT provided better forecast for a time series with high vari-ability. However, TFT is confirmed to be computationally expensive, as shown by its computational time compared to GRU, and LSTM.
This work has several important limitations. First, this study relied solely on historical price data, which may overlook critical exogenous drivers such as weather fluctuations, holiday demand spikes (for exam-ple, around Eid or year-end celebrations), and global commodity price trends. Second, the superior accuracy of TFT comes at the cost of sub-stantially longer training and inference times compared to GRU and LSTM baselines, which could impede real-time deployment in resource-constrained settings.
To address these gaps, three primary directions for future research are proposed. First, future works can extend to multivariate inputs by incorporating climate variables, supply-chain indicators, and event cal-endars in a TFT framework, to assess whether forecast accuracy and robustness improve when key exogenous factors are modeled explic-itly. Second, TFT can be compared against Multi-Input Multi-Output (MIMO) forecasting architectures to determine whether simpler models can approach TFT’s performance while reducing computational over-head. Third, generalizability needs to be validated by applying multi-variate TFT to data from different regions and to periods of extreme price volatility, such as during global crises, to evaluate model stability and transferability.
By systematically tackling these limitations, TFT’s practical utility in the food commodities sector can be refined and establish a foundation for more transparent, eﬃcient, and generalizable multi-horizon fore-casting models.
